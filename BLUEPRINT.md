## Executive summary

- **What this is:** the AI+1 Blueprint for *AI Filmmaking*. It is a self-paced online course for a trained filmmaker who already has a finished screenplay and is new to AI. It is technical: how to take that screenplay and turn it into something an agent can make into a film. Each chapter starts from craft the filmmaker already has and covers three things:
  - what the model needs to produce it;
  - what the model does by default, and how to override that;
  - what only the filmmaker can judge.
- **Two courses, two repos (Bear, 2026-11-17):** this one, *AI Filmmaking*, is for filmmakers like Galen and is the one being worked on. The other, *Agentic Filmmaking*, is for a general audience without a film background, who may know ChatGPT and a little Midjourney or Higgsfield. It spends much more time on story and filmmaking, and on why they matter. Its starting plan is in [/Users/bear/Documents/CoWork/bear-textbooks/books/agentic-filmmaking-book](/Users/bear/Documents/CoWork/bear-textbooks/books/agentic-filmmaking-book).
- **The thesis (Bear, 2026-11-16):** the filmmaker conducts; the agent plays. The agent generates, logs and prices options. Every choice of what to keep, reject or change belongs to the filmmaker.
- **The structure:** an introduction, 13 chapters in four parts, and a capstone. The old Chapters 1 to 3 (film language, story, screenplay) are now one chapter: the agent's notes on a finished screenplay and its conversion into a shot table. The chapters run in cost order: words, then stills (cheap drafts first, then paid hero stills), then motion and sound, then the cut.

## Concept

The other is *"for AI people who do not know how to make a film."* This course is the first: *"Galen has a master's degree in film. He has a screenplay, and he needs to convert that screenplay into a format that can be made into film. So it's more technical."*

Bear, later the same day, splitting the work into two repos: this course is for *"somebody who already knows filmmaking but does not know AI. He also already has a script."* It is for anybody in Galen's situation, who has spent more time raising money for a dream film than making it. Human judgment is critical in both courses; the difference is that *"Galen has a vision in his head already, and he needs to get the AI tools to help him get as close to his vision as possible."* [CONFIRMED: Bear, 2026-11-17]

Thesis: the filmmaker conducts; the agent plays. The agent generates, logs and prices options; every choice of what to keep, reject or change belongs to the filmmaker. The proof is that two people can take the same idea, run the same tools and make completely different films, because the difference lives in their decisions, not in the software. The course teaches the tech so it stops being the obstacle, and never makes the creative calls for the reader.

**Two consequences that run through the introduction and every chapter's AI half:**

- **The agent is the hands.** What the course removes is the human translator standing between the filmmaker's vision and the agent, not the delegation. The pitch is "direct it yourself", not "do it yourself".
- **Bear's role ends at teaching.** Bear will not be Galen's hands, or relay "change this, update that" between Galen and the agent. The course exists so the filmmaker talks to the agent directly.

**The question behind it** (from [/Users/bear/Documents/CoWork/bear-textbooks/books/ai-filmmaking/README.md](/Users/bear/Documents/CoWork/bear-textbooks/books/ai-filmmaking/README.md)): can a filmmaker with no Hollywood budget now make the film they have carried for years? Galen has spent most of his time raising money. The realistic first win is a finished opening scene that helps raise money for the rest. That is what the capstone builds.

Target reader: a trained filmmaker who already has a finished screenplay and is new to AI. They know story, conflict, script format, coverage and storyboards. What they lack is the technical path from that screenplay to something an agent can generate. Galen is both the target reader and the running case: he has a master's degree in film and a full script. The translation from the filmmaker's craft to what a model needs is what separates this course from tool tutorials. [CONFIRMED: Bear, 2026-11-17, after a call with Galen]

**Deployment: a self-paced online course, with no fixed length.** Not a semester course, not a workshop. In Bear's words, it is for people who *"have a dream of making films"* and want to start making their vision *without "going down a rabbit hole of bad decisions"*. [CONFIRMED: Bear, 2026-11-16]

**Writing rule:** plain and practical. Every AI term is defined in plain words the first time it appears. Film terms are used the way a filmmaker uses them, and are not taught again.

## Chapter anatomy (every chapter, same five parts)

1. **From your craft to the model.** It starts from the craft the filmmaker already has: shot size, lens, light quality, period detail, coverage. Then it shows what the model needs to produce it:
   - the words the model follows;
   - the words it ignores (found by testing, never assumed);
   - the job settings that carry the rest (first frame, references, aspect ratio, length).

   This course is technical, and film craft is not taught again.
2. **The agent does it.** What the model does by default, and how to override it. The agent is the hands; the filmmaker directs it directly, in their own words. Model and tool names appear only in a dated **"Tools as of"** box, so the chapter does not age with them.
3. The +1. What only the filmmaker can judge: where the output departs from what the filmmaker intended. Named failures come from real work (a ghost second sail, a spyglass where the script says monocle). A departure from history, or from someone else's taste, is not a failure: the agent flags it once, the filmmaker decides, and the decision goes in the production log.
4. **On the set: Galen's opening.** What was actually done so far, with the real files, and whose hands did it. Where something has not been run yet, the chapter gives Galen the exact session to run, never a description of what he would decide.
5. **Exercises.** At least three; one at Apply level or higher. Every exercise ends in a file the student can open. Templates have blanks for the reader's own decisions; there are no finished prompts to copy. Galen's prompts are shown as examples of his decisions, never as defaults for the reader.

## Sequencing model and three-act arc

**Sequencing model: the cost curve.** The course follows the production order (development, pre-production, production, post, release), which every pantry book also uses. The agentic twist is that this order is also the cost order. A word costs almost nothing. A logged still costs about 2 credits. A logged 8-second clip costs 26 to 128 credits, depending on the model. Generation costs real money. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: dollar price per credit, so the course can state costs in money.] Bear's first rule says it: *work from cheap to expensive; fix problems at the still, because a bad still makes a bad clip.* So the frame, the light and the look move forward into pre-production, though they are classically production work. They are decided on stills before any motion is paid for.

Within the stills there are two tiers. Explore with fast, cheap image models (draft stills); pay for a high-quality model only for the hero still that becomes a clip's first frame, because the video can only be as clear as the still it starts from. The rule students carry: never pay for motion to find out what something looks like. Worked example: the Wrong Ship. A trireme that a draft still would have exposed for a few credits reached two finished films before it was caught. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: credits spent on Films 1 and 2 before the ship was caught.]

[NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: logged price per draft still and per hero still, so Ch 7 can budget the two tiers separately.]

**Anchor exhibit: the Wrong Ship.** Asked for an ancient Greek ship, the generation went to a textbook trireme with two sails and banks of oars, and that ship appeared in Films 1 and 2. Galen's ship, as the later stills show, has a curl and a beak and a single bank of oars. Only he could catch the difference. The notes on the review slides are the relay loop this course is designed to end:
- "whole ship · curl and beak · adults"
- "warm wood, oars biting, too many aboard"
- "same hull · no lightning"

In the course, notes like these are what the filmmaker types to the agent directly. The exhibit appears in the introduction, in Ch 5, and in the cost chapter (Ch 7).

**Three-act arc:**

- **Act One: Establish (Intro and Ch 1).** What is the plan? The filmmaker gets the agent's notes on the finished screenplay and converts it into a shot table the agent can generate from.
- **Act Two: Build (Ch 2 to Ch 10).** The hard middle. First the whole film as stills (look, frame, light, world, storyboard, budget), then motion, performance and sound. Ch 7 (Producing) is the gate in the middle: nothing moves until the stills are approved and the money is planned.
- **Act Three: Apply (Ch 11 to Ch 13).** The filmmaker cuts, finishes and releases a film, with credits, disclosure and rights in order.
- **Capstone.** The opening scene of the filmmaker's own screenplay, made through the whole pipeline, with a production log.

Arc statement: the course takes the filmmaker from *a finished screenplay* to *a finished, honestly labelled opening scene they directed*. It does this in three steps: convert the screenplay into a plan the agent can generate from; see the whole film in cheap draft stills; then pay for hero stills and motion only where the drafts have earned it.
