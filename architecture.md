## Learning Outcomes

Format: by the end of the chapter, the filmmaker will be able to...

**Introduction: Can You Make Your Film Now?**
- (Understand) explain who does what: the agent generates, logs and prices options; the filmmaker keeps, rejects or changes them.
- (Understand) describe the cost curve (words, draft stills, hero stills, motion, sound, cut) and why you never pay for motion to find out what something looks like.
- (Apply) set up a project folder, a production log and an agent session that record every decision, cost and job ID.
- (Apply) make a three-shot micro-film end to end and log what departed from what they intended. *(the pebble; see Three-Act Learning Arc)*

**Ch 1: From Screenplay to Shot Table**
- (Analyze) explain what a model needs that a screenplay leaves to the crew (picture, sound, length, camera, references, continuity), using a page of their own script.
- (Evaluate) answer each note the agent raises on their screenplay (format, continuity inside the script, period, rights) with a logged decision, and decline notes that pull the script toward the familiar.
- (Create) convert their screenplay into a shot table the agent can generate from, using a template with blanks for their own decisions.
- (Evaluate) argue whether a generated image can do what Bazin claimed for the photographic image, using one example.

**Ch 2: The Visual Story**
- (Create) write look rules (light, palette, camera, what is banned in frame) that override the model's default look in every prompt.
- (Create) write a note for each sequence saying where the visual intensity rises and falls, since the model remembers nothing between shots.
- (Analyze) compare the same scene made under opposite look rules, or by two people, and name the decisions that made them differ.
- (Evaluate) judge a set of generated frames against their own look rules.

**Ch 3: The Frame: Composition, Angle and Lens**
- (Apply) specify a frame by shot size, angle, lens and depth of field, splitting the prompt words from the job settings (aspect ratio, resolution, length).
- (Analyze) test which camera words two image models follow and which they ignore, and record the result.
- (Evaluate) decide whether a still matches the frame they intended, and keep or reject it with a reason.

**Ch 4: Light and Color**
- (Apply) describe each light by its source, direction, quality, time of day and color, precisely enough for a model to produce it.
- (Create) write a lighting and color scheme for a scene as a fixed description carried across a sequence.
- (Apply) hold that scheme across a sequence of draft stills and repair the stills that drift.

**Ch 5: Designing the World: Sets, Props, Costume and Characters**
- (Create) build a character reference sheet (four or five angles) and a hero-prop reference.
- (Analyze) find where generated designs depart from the filmmaker's intent (identity drift, wrong details, continuity).
- (Evaluate) flag where a design departs from the period, and record each departure as the filmmaker's choice.
- (Evaluate) decide whether a likeness may be used, based on documented consent.

**Ch 6: Storyboards and the Shot List**
- (Apply) storyboard a whole scene in draft stills from the shot table, numbered, with labels and a map.
- (Evaluate) review the storyboard frame by frame against their intent, logging each keep, reject or question.
- (Create) choose which frames get hero stills to become first frames, and write the linked shot list.

**Ch 7: Producing: Budget, Schedule and Crew**
- (Apply) break the script down into production elements priced in credits per still and per second of motion.
- (Create) build a shot-by-shot budget that prices draft stills, hero stills and motion separately, with a test budget.
- (Evaluate) decide which shots to generate, shoot live, or cut, and justify each against budget and story.

**Ch 8: Movement: Camera and Action**
- (Apply) generate clips from approved hero stills, each with one stated camera move that overrides the model's default motion.
- (Analyze) test which camera-move words a video model follows, and record the result.
- (Evaluate) review clips on four-frame contact sheets and reject motion that departs from their intent.

**Ch 9: Directing Performance and Coverage**
- (Apply) plan coverage for a two-character dialogue scene with a floor plan and a stated side of the line in every prompt.
- (Create) generate that coverage with consistent characters and matching eyelines, drafts before hero stills before motion.
- (Evaluate) judge performances across takes and choose one, with reasons.

**Ch 10: Sound: Dialogue, Voice, Effects and Music**
- (Apply) produce voice, ambience, effects and music for a scene and have the agent mix them to their loudness target.
- (Create) write a sound list for a scene, beat by beat, as the spec the agent works from.
- (Evaluate) keep a rights and consent record for every voice and music source, and reject any source that fails it.

**Ch 11: Editing: Continuity, Time and Rhythm**
- (Apply) direct the agent to assemble a scene from the shot table.
- (Create) cut the assembly to a fine cut by giving cut notes to the agent directly.
- (Evaluate) justify each cut against Murch's rule of six.

**Ch 12: VFX, Compositing and Finishing**
- (Apply) composite a generated element into a live-action plate, or a live element into a generated shot.
- (Apply) finish a cut: titles and overlays added in post, upscaling last.
- (Evaluate) label, shot by shot, what is real and what is generated.

**Ch 13: Distribution, Festivals, Pitching and Disclosure**
- (Apply) prepare delivery files, captions, metadata and synthetic-media disclosure for a release.
- (Create) build a festival and fundraising plan, with a pitch and a pitch reel.
- (Evaluate) check a film's credits and rights record against festival and platform rules.

**Capstone: The Opening Scene**
- (Create) make and release the opening scene of their own screenplay through the whole pipeline.
- (Evaluate) check the film and the production log against the self-review checklist: every decision, cost, rejected take and disclosure.

## Sequencing Model and Justification

**Primary model: simple to complex, ordered by cost.** Each part adds one expensive dimension to the film:
- Part I: words (the screenplay converted).
- Part II: space and light (stills).
- Part III: time and sound (clips).
- Part IV: the whole (the cut and the release).

Within Part II, stills come in two tiers: cheap draft stills to explore, then one paid hero still per approved shot, because the video can only be as clear as the still it starts from. A filmmaker who pays for credits makes the cheapest mistakes first. This is how the course keeps them out of the rabbit hole of bad decisions.

**Secondary model: a spiral of the review pass.** The +1 judgment returns in every part, each time against the filmmaker's intent, with a new layer:

| Return | Chapter | What is judged | New layer this time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ch 1 | the agent's notes on the screenplay, and the shot table | meaning: is the script still yours, and does every row say what you meant? |
| 2 | Ch 2 to 6 | draft stills | space, light and identity in one frame; period departures logged as choices |
| 3 | Ch 8 | clips | time and physics across the frames of a clip |
| 4 | Ch 9 | coverage | identity, eyeline and screen direction across shots |
| 5 | Ch 11 | the cut | rhythm and emotion across the whole scene, through the filmmaker's own cut notes |

**Where the model is most likely to break:** Ch 9. Directing performance needs everything before it, and it is where the models are weakest and where no real run exists yet.

**Transition chapter:** Ch 7 (Producing), the pivot from cheap to expensive work. Before crossing it, the filmmaker has reviewed draft stills, chosen hero stills, look rules, character references and a budget.

## Three-Act Learning Arc

**Length:** self-paced with no fixed length, so the 12-to-14-chapter semester target does not apply. This course has 13 chapters plus an Introduction. [Follows from Bear's deployment answer, 2026-11-16, and the merge of old Ch 1 to 3, 2026-11-17]

**The pebble (Merrill's first principle).** Recommended: in the Introduction, before any theory, the filmmaker makes a three-shot micro-film with the agent from a few lines of their own screenplay. It has a few draft stills, one hero still, one clip, and a cut with a voice line. They see the whole pipeline once, and see where it departs from what they intended, which creates the need for every chapter after. At logged prices that is about 2 credits per draft still plus 26 for the cheapest logged clip, plus the hero still. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT: hero-still price] [INFERRED — Blueprint recommendation]

**Act One: Establish (Intro and Ch 1).**
- *Inciting question:* can the screenplay I already have become a plan an agent can generate from, before I spend anything?
- *By the end:* a shot table, with a logged decision for every note the agent raised.

**Act Two: Build (Ch 2 to Ch 10).**
- *Tools it provides:* look rules, the frame and light words the model follows, character references, the draft storyboard and its review, hero stills, the budget, image-to-video, coverage, the mix.
- *Hardest moment:* Ch 9, consistent performance across generated coverage.
- *By the end:* every shot of the scene exists as an approved clip with its sound, inside the budget.

**Act Three: Apply (Ch 11 to Ch 13, capstone).**
- *Where it comes together:* the cut, the finish, the release.
- *What the filmmaker produces:* a released opening scene, honestly labelled, with its production log; the first win that can help raise money for the rest.

**Transitions:**
- *Act One to Act Two:* the shot table exists and every note on the screenplay has a logged decision.
- *Act Two to Act Three:* approved clips and a soundtrack exist, within budget, with job IDs logged.

**Arc statement:** the course takes the filmmaker from *a finished screenplay* to *a finished, honestly labelled opening scene they directed*. It does this in three steps: convert the screenplay into a plan the agent can generate from; see the whole film in cheap draft stills; then pay for hero stills and motion only where the drafts have earned it.

## Front-Loading Decisions

- **Introduction as Chapter 0:** agent setup, project folder, production log, accounts and credits, and the pebble.
- **Film craft is assumed, not taught.** AI terms (draft still, hero still, first frame, reference, job settings) are defined at first use, in plain words, each beside the film term it maps to. Audio terms specific to generated sound get an embedded explanation in Ch 10, not a chapter.
- **Editing software:** not required; the agent assembles. A desktop editor is optional. Trained filmmakers may already use one; the course does not teach it. [NEEDS HUMAN INPUT — name one editor, or none]
